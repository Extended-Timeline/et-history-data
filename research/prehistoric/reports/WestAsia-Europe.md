# West Asia and Europe before 3000 BCE: research synthesis

## Scope and method

This file records selected, dateable archaeological evidence from the Paleolithic through the Neolithic and Chalcolithic in West/Southwest Asia and Europe. It is a regional evidence layer, not an attempt to assign every province an owner or to draw prehistoric borders. The 22 records concern sites, settlement sequences, material traditions, subsistence shifts, and monuments. Their findings remain archaeological interpretations: Natufian, Halaf, Ubaid, LBK, Cardial, and Cucuteni–Trypillia are not asserted to be ethnicities, languages, religions, or states.

The sources are 16 UNESCO/heritage, museum, municipal archaeological, and peer-reviewed research references in [westasia_europe_sources.yml](../regions/westasia_europe_sources.yml). Each factual YAML record points to a source and locator. UNESCO dossiers are especially useful for site dates, physical remains, and conservation context; the Metropolitan Museum chronology and essay support regional pottery and Ubaid descriptions; peer-reviewed studies underpin the Zagros goat-management evidence and the Cardial/LBK dispersal summary. Heritage summaries are broad chronologies, so claims that depend on exact phasing have lower confidence or explicitly note uncertainty.

Dates reported as BCE are represented as negative year values in the regional YAML. Dates originally published in years BP remain in BP form, with the reference year and calibration state recorded; they have not been converted into BCE. The Atapuerca source says “nearly one million years” ago, so that record preserves a relative date without assigning an unstated reference year. Broad millennium dates and approximate starts retain `approximate: true`. Long sequences that cross 3000 BCE retain the reported full interval and carry a separate research window ending before the cutoff.

## Chronological phases and evidence

**Paleolithic occupation and human evolution.** The Mount Carmel sequence is a key long record spanning the Acheulean, Mousterian, and Natufian periods. UNESCO reports the Mousterian as 250,000–45,000 years BP and notes Neanderthals and early anatomically modern humans in the same Middle Paleolithic cultural framework; it does not specify whether those dates are calibrated. The Atapuerca caves are described as an exceptional record of early human occupation in Europe extending back “nearly one million years.” This remains a relative years-ago statement, not a converted BP or BCE interval. These establish that a prehistoric timeline begins vastly earlier than the conventional “civilization” era, but both are long cave/site sequences. Neither identifies a continuous population, polity, or territory. [Mount Carmel caves](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1393) · [Atapuerca](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/989/)

**Late Epipaleolithic and early Neolithic Southwest Asia.** The Natufian record at Mount Carmel captures an important transition: stone houses and pits indicate built activity and increasingly sedentary hunter-gatherer communities, while agriculture and animal husbandry belong to a later shift. Tell es-Sultan/Jericho has human activity from about 10,500 BCE beside a perennial spring; by the ninth to eighth millennia BCE it had a substantial permanent Neolithic settlement with a wall, ditch, and tower. The sequence demonstrates sedentism and coordinated construction, not an early city-state (the better-known urban evidence belongs to the Bronze Age and is excluded). Göbekli Tepe’s monumental structures, dated 9600–8200 BCE, were built by hunter-gatherers in the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. This is strong evidence that monumental construction did not require an agricultural state. [Mount Carmel](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1393) · [Tell es-Sultan](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1687) · [Göbekli Tepe](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1572/)

Central Anatolia’s Çatalhöyük sequence is deliberately split by mound: UNESCO places Neolithic occupation on the East Mound at 7400–6200 BCE and Chalcolithic occupation on the West Mound at approximately 6200–5200 BCE. Both intervals fall before the 3000 BCE cutoff. In the Zagros, genomic and zooarchaeological evidence from Ganj Dareh and Tepe Abdul Hosein supports management of genetically domestic goats by about 8200 cal BCE, while visible morphological change appeared later. This distinction matters for timeline claims: “herding/management” can predate clear skeletal domestication markers. [Çatalhöyük](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1405) · [Zagros goat study](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2100901118)

**Mesopotamia, Iran, and Cyprus.** Northern Mesopotamian Hassuna, Samarra, and Halaf pottery traditions are treated as material-culture evidence with broad sixth-millennium ranges, not political units. The Halaf tradition’s distribution from the Mediterranean toward the Zagros foothills signals regional interaction; the source does not warrant a territorial map. In southern Mesopotamia, Ubaid villages emerged around 6200 BCE on alluvial plains, combining farming, irrigation, and fishing. Ubaid material culture spread north, and the museum chronology ends around 4000 BCE, fully before the research cutoff. Cyprus’ Choirokoitia provides evidence for permanent occupation from around 7000 BCE in an aceramic Neolithic setting; proposed origins among settlers from Anatolia or the Levant remain an interpretation rather than a certain route. [Metropolitan Museum Mesopotamia chronology](https://www.metmuseum.org/ru/toah/ht/02/wam.html) · [Ubaid essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/ja/essays/the-ubaid-period-5500-4000-b-c) · [Choirokoitia](https://whc.unesco.org/document/154428)

**Spread and regionalization of farming in Europe.** The reviewed Cardial/LBK study describes an early Balkan expansion followed by two broad routes around 6000–5500 cal BCE: an Impressa/Cardial route along Mediterranean coasts and an inland Linearbandkeramik (LBK) route along the Danube into Central Europe. These are useful high-level dispersal patterns, but they are not uniform “civilization” events: the study discusses migration and coastal pioneer settlement as interpretations, and local farmer–forager interaction varied. At Lepenski Vir and other Iron Gates sites, occupation from roughly 9500 to 5500 BCE encompasses Mesolithic and Early Neolithic phases and contact with incoming farming communities. For Britain, English Heritage places the arrival of farming communities from continental Europe around 4000 BCE; Stonehenge’s broader landscape evidence includes long barrows, cursus monuments, and causewayed enclosures around 3500 BCE, with an earthwork at Stonehenge around 3000 BCE. The final date sits on the period boundary, and later Stonehenge phases are outside this study. [Cardial/LBK study](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msv181) · [Lepenski Vir](https://geoparkdjerdap.rs/en/kulturno_nasledje/lepenski-vir/) · [Stonehenge timeline](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/history-and-stories/timeline)

**Chalcolithic complexity and monument building.** Varna’s cemetery (approximately 4600–4200 BCE) documents varied burial practices and richly furnished graves, including early substantial gold artifacts. This supports a statement about social differentiation in burial evidence, but not a named ruler, government, or polity. Cucuteni–Trypillia settlement expansion across parts of present-day Romania, Moldova, and Ukraine is recorded between approximately 4900 and 4050 BCE; UNESCO notes siting near water, cultivable land, forests, and raw materials. Modern borders are only geographic reference points. On Malta, UNESCO dates monumental temples across the fourth and third millennia BCE. The regional record retains this entire reported span and marks only its pre-3000 BCE portion as within the research window; it avoids asserting a specific religion or purpose. [Varna](https://visit.varna.bg/en/varna_oldest_gold_treasure_in_the_world.html) · [Cucuteni–Trypillia](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/7009/) · [Maltese temples](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/132/)

## Coverage and interpretation limits

Coverage includes the requested Anatolian, Levantine, Mesopotamian/Iranian, continental European, and British contexts. It is intentionally selected rather than globally or locally exhaustive. Regional evidence is uneven: West Asia has more early Neolithic settlement and domestication anchors; continental Europe has representative dispersal and contact evidence; Britain is represented by a broad farming arrival and a major monument landscape. The Balkans/Black Sea, Iberia, Mediterranean islands, and northern Europe could support additional local sequences, but adding them requires more site-specific dating and research than these sources provide.

Evidence in this set can support game fields describing approximate dated site activity, broad subsistence transition, archaeological material traditions, settlement form, and monument/building activity. It does not support named prehistoric states, sovereignty or province ownership, language or religion assignments, ruler lists, army sizes, urban population totals, or exact demographic estimates. Even conspicuous sites can be intermittent, ritual, seasonal, or only partially excavated; dates for a culture or mound cannot safely stand in for every location within its distribution. Where a site name maps to a game location, that mapping should be a separate, reviewable decision based on verified coordinates and the game's geography. No location IDs are assigned in this research pass.

Population estimates are omitted because none of the reviewed sources supplies a methodologically comparable count suitable for game data. The large-site label at Jericho, for example, does not license a numerical population. Likewise, archaeological density or burial wealth cannot be converted directly into province population or state capacity. More research would be needed before adding numeric population, state tags, and province-wide culture or religion values.

## Priorities for later research

The next useful expansion would add source-backed local sequences for the Iranian plateau and northern Mesopotamia, the Aegean and Balkans, Iberia and western Mediterranean islands, and northern/eastern Europe, then check all dates against radiocarbon datasets and regional phase definitions. For gameplay integration, the essential next step is to match selected sites to verified map locations while preserving the distinction between a point site, a cultural distribution, and a political claim. Any country or province-wide assignment should require independent evidence beyond this archaeological site layer.
