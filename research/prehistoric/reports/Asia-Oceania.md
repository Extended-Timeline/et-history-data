# Asia and Oceania: prehistoric evidence through 3000 BCE

## Scope and method

This regional survey selects archaeological evidence from first settlement and Paleolithic sequences through farming and technological change before 3000 BCE. It is not a complete gazetteer. It uses archaeological sites and assemblages as evidence, not as proxies for ethnic, linguistic or political identities. Years in BCE are negative calendar years in the data; published BP dates remain in their source scale and are not converted unless the source itself supplies a calendar estimate. Approximate and debated chronologies are labeled. Any interval continuing after 3000 BCE remains intact as `reported_range`, while this class should only use the portion ending at the cutoff.

## Chronological phases and regional evidence

**Pleistocene settlement and adaptation.** South Asia provides evidence for repeated occupation, but interpretation is uneven. At Jwalapuram in southern India, Middle Palaeolithic artefacts occur below and above ash associated with the roughly 74 ka Toba eruption. Similarity across the ash supports continuity of occupation, but does not establish who made the tools or prove an uninterrupted population history. Dhaba in the Middle Son Valley offers a separate northern sequence spanning the eruption and later lithic changes. These are stone-tool records; neither warrants a named population, migration route, polity or exact population total. The primary Fa-Hien Lena bone-tool study adds an assemblage interpreted as projectile technology and symbolic activity reaching about 48,000 BP. The full dating tables still need review before precise calendar mapping. [Fa-Hien Lena study](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aba3831)

In northern Australia, Madjedbebe excavations have been interpreted as evidence of occupation by about 65 ± 6 ka. That chronology is influential and debated, so it is recorded as an interval and not as an exact arrival date. Lake Mungo provides a distinct mortuary record: the National Museum of Australia dates Mungo Lady to 40,000–42,000 years ago and describes cremation. This demonstrates deliberate treatment of the dead, not a specific doctrine or belief. In Baikal Siberia, the museum essay places Mal’ta’s bone-and-antler house architecture around 20,000 BCE and illustrates a mammoth-ivory figurine around 21,000 BCE. [Mal’ta essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/malta-ca-20000-b-c) These observations establish archaeological presence and technical activity without implying a bounded culture held one fixed identity across millennia.

**Holocene villages, pottery and cultivation.** Lower Yangtze Shangshan contexts show rice use and harvesting, alongside early pottery, by around 10,000 calibrated BP. Research treats domestication as a long process; the record should not turn the earliest plant use into a claim for fully domesticated rice or a uniform farming economy. Jiahu in Henan offers evidence of rice and pottery, as well as chemical evidence for a fermented rice, honey and fruit drink in pottery residues dated about 7000–6600 BCE. Residue analysis is a strong observation about specific vessels, but it does not demonstrate broad production or trade.

Japan’s Jomon tradition is identified through pottery and includes long-lived communities whose subsistence cannot be reduced to a farming transition. Tokyo National Museum dates a Natsushima vessel to 7000–4000 BCE, an interval fully before the cutoff. Korea’s National Museum gives a very broad peninsula-wide Neolithic span of about 8000–1500 BCE, characterized by stone and bone tools and early pottery. These broad labels conceal local diversity and must not be converted into a single synchronized onset or political unit.

Mehrgarh’s UNESCO tentative-list phase table describes an aceramic settlement, later pottery and a village craft zone across approximately 7000–4500 BCE. These are archaeological phases with approximate dates, not a state or a simple linear predecessor of the Indus cities. The wider Indus synthesis distinguishes food-producing and regionalization phases; their geography and subsistence need local review.

At Jeitun in southern Turkmenistan, excavation syntheses report rectangular houses, ceramics, barley, wheat and sheep, and argue that settled agriculture entered this part of Central Asia from outside. That is evidence for a local village economy and regional transmission, not a precise migration path. In mainland Southeast Asia, Hoabinhian is best used as a variable lithic technocomplex, not a people. Laang Spean in Cambodia and Spirit Cave in Thailand show stratified forager records that persist into periods with pottery, ground stone and plant remains. At Spirit Cave, cultivation is presented as a possibility rather than a demonstrated fact; mixed subsistence is the safer interpretation.

**Local agricultural innovation and complex centers.** Kuk Swamp in the New Guinea Highlands provides particularly important evidence against a single global pathway to farming. Wetland manipulation and plant exploitation reach back to roughly 10,000 cal BP, but whether the earliest phase qualifies as agriculture is disputed. Mounded cultivation dated around 6950–6440 cal BP is described by the ANU synthesis as the earliest unequivocal evidence of agriculture at Kuk. A shift toward cultivation does not in itself imply urbanism, hierarchy or state institutions. By contrast, Liangzhu in the lower Yangtze (about 3300–2300 BCE) is described by UNESCO as an early regional center with rice cultivation, a planned urban complex, major waterworks, settlement hierarchy, jade ritual objects and social differentiation. Its evidence is especially useful for mapping a complex settlement node, but its interval crosses the 3000 BCE class boundary: preserve the full source chronology while clipping this class's event evidence at 3000 BCE.

The Pacific settlement boundary needs explicit handling. Lapita-associated initial colonization of Remote Oceania is conventionally placed around 3000 years ago, long after 3000 BCE; settlement of Polynesia and Eastern Polynesia came later still. Therefore the absence of pre-3000 BCE Remote Oceania entries here means that this sampled archaeological record does not support extending settlement dates backward. It does not claim that all Pacific islands have equally complete archaeological coverage.

## Evidence limits and implications for game data

The strongest evidence concerns dated sites, tools, pottery, plant remains, built environments and selected mortuary contexts. Archaeological visibility varies sharply: caves, dry uplands and preserved wetlands often provide sequences, while tropical lowlands, coastlines and submerged landscapes remain harder to reconstruct. Farming evidence is also not a single threshold. Rice cultivation in China, village farming in Mehrgarh and Jeitun, mixed forager economies in Southeast Asia, and wetland cultivation in New Guinea have distinct chronologies and evidentiary standards. Do not apply a European Neolithic date globally.

The reviewed sources do not support numeric population estimates, named rulers, territorial borders, political control, language identities or specific religions for the Paleolithic and most early farming records. Trade should only be entered where source evidence directly demonstrates provenance or exchange; ornament materials and stylistic similarities alone are not enough for routes in this dataset. Where institutions are not archaeologically demonstrated, leave state and governance fields empty. In game-history integration, prioritize broad site presence and supported regional resource/technology developments; attach dates to sites or phases with stated uncertainty, keep BP provenance visible, and avoid inventing country tags or continuous control. The Asia–Oceania records are a research layer for later mapping, not ready-made territorial history.
