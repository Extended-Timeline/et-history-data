# Native EU5 history requirements: politics, institutions and social organization

## Scope and method

This starter batch derives research fields from the native EU5 political setup, category definitions, and dated setup structures. It asks what a source-based historical record should describe; it does not claim that the project already supports those fields or that EU5 abstractions are universal historical categories. Native line references are relative to `D:/Games/Europa Universalis 5/Europa.Universalis.V.v1.2.5-0xdeadcode/game`. The inspected scenario separates territorial control (`main_menu/setup/start/10_countries.txt:1-13`), country/government setup (`:15` onward), characters and dynasties (`05_characters.txt`, `04_dynasties.txt`), diplomacy (`12_diplomacy.txt`), international organizations (`15_international_organizations.txt`) and wars (`16_wars.txt`).

The requirements are about two complementary kinds of evidence. A snapshot records who or what held a status at a point or interval: territorial actor, government, law, office-holder, social group, alliance, subject relation or active conflict. A process record captures transitions: emergence and disappearance, expansion or loss of control, institutional change, succession, alliance or tribute changes, and conflict. Political chronology should preserve gaps rather than connect separate observations into an unsupported continuous state.

## Twenty-five requirements for historical data

The accompanying YAML lists 25 requirements and their native anchors. Territorial history should distinguish claims, cores, ownership and effective control; the native file itself comments separate categories for own/control and core/integrated/conquered/colony. Archaeological distributions, language areas and shared artefact horizons do not establish these boundaries. An entity record needs its own evidence-based identity, start/end estimates and relationship to predecessor or successor entities. Formation, dissolution, annexation, political fragmentation and cultural continuity are distinct propositions.

Government history should describe supported political arrangements and separately dated institutional changes. The native setup provides a government type and heir-selection field for Sweden (`10_countries.txt:68-72`), while common definitions enumerate monarchies, republics, theocracies and tribes. Government reforms and laws are separate vocabulary. An archaeological settlement, monumental project, farming economy or mortuary disparity alone does not establish any one of these forms. Likewise, societal-value axes such as centralization versus decentralization (`common/societal_values/00_default.txt:1`) are game parameters; research must record the observed evidence first and keep a later game mapping as an interpretation.

Social organization deserves its own evidence layer. Native estates and privileges describe corporate influence and legal or economic position, but archaeological status patterns do not automatically identify nobles, clergy or peasants. Record evidence for differentiated access, labor, ritual roles, obligations, prestige and wealth separately, with sample and site scope. Where a hierarchy is proposed, state which evidence supports it and what remains disputed. Do not encode a modern state apparatus simply because the settlement has public architecture or an organized ritual center.

Named people require unusually strict controls. Native person records can carry names, life dates, origin and estate; country setup can identify a ruler, consort and dated ruler terms. Native dynasties connect people to named houses, while succession laws select among heirs. Historical records should model a ruler or kin link only when a source identifies that person or relationship. Genetic affinity, shared burial context and repeated rebuilding can support a bounded kinship observation, but none supplies a complete genealogy, dynasty, office succession or named king by itself. For prehistory, an unknown ruler is a valid state of knowledge and must remain unknown.

Diplomacy and conflict require party identity, relationship direction, timing and terms. The native diplomacy file distinguishes dependencies by first/second party, subject type and optional start date (`12_diplomacy.txt:7-20`); its alliance categories are not inferred from material exchange. International organizations (`15_international_organizations.txt`) and wars (`16_wars.txt:1-32`) similarly need membership or belligerent evidence. Exchange does not prove tribute; cultural similarity does not prove alliance; fortification or trauma alone does not identify a war, aggressor, state, or border. When parties cannot be named, store that uncertainty rather than manufacturing tags.

## Prehistoric evidence batch: Hongshan social processes

This starter research focuses on Hongshan communities in northeastern China, roughly 4500–3000 BCE, and addresses three processes rather than listing archaeological culture names as countries. A regional-scale study describes Niuheliang as a late stage of ceremonial investment and interprets it and select sub-localities as mechanisms of social, political and regional stratification around the approximate mid-fourth millennium BCE. The authors discuss landscape position, routes, geology, visibility, architecture and portable goods. This is an interpretive process model, not evidence for a state, ruler or mapped frontier; the date remains relative as reported.

A household assemblage study of 69 units reports modest prestige variation, similar living standards, very low productive differentiation and strongest differentiation in ritual/religious roles. This is a period-spanning comparison, not evidence for a transition across time; it complicates assumptions that monumental ritual architecture implies wealth-based class hierarchy or strong centralized government. The 4500–3000 BCE reported period is retained whole as boundary context.

A 2026 geochemical study of 231 sherds from six Late Hongshan household units at Dongshanzui identifies limited local clay sources and interprets a few specialized households as supplying a broader community; status is linked to preferential access, not production control. This sample does not date the emergence of specialization or establish taxation, tribute or political territory. Its full 3500–3000 BCE range is preserved as boundary context.

Taken together, these studies support richer descriptions of regional ceremonial centrality, ritual roles, household differentiation, access and production relationships, while keeping territorial authority and named rulers unassigned. This is a starter batch, not global prehistoric coverage. The next research pass should test comparable questions region by region, favor excavated contexts and explicit methods, and avoid turning social-science labels into direct game government IDs.

## Data priorities

For historical coverage, first gather dated evidence for political entities and territory with explicit distinctions among claimed, controlled and incorporated areas. Next add institutional events: government and law changes, social differentiation, named office holders and succession only where sources identify them. Then record bilateral diplomacy, dependency or tribute, multi-polity institutions, and wars as time-bounded relationships with named or explicitly unknown parties. For each feature, capture source locator, dating system and precision, site or regional scope, confidence and competing interpretation. Keep game categories and numerical axes in a separate mapping layer so that engine design never masquerades as historical evidence.
