# Africa and the Americas before 3000 BCE

## Evidence and chronological limits

This compilation contains 25 evidence records. It separates human-evolution evidence from archaeological evidence about populations and material traditions. Middle Stone Age, Naqada, and Clovis describe material evidence and chronology; they are not countries, ethnicities, languages, or single political actors. Dates retain source conventions: calibrated BP, unspecified years ago, and calendar BCE are not interchangeable. Long-running records reach the 3000 BCE boundary; their dates are preserved rather than clipped.

Research visibility is uneven. Fossils and caves preserve strong evidence for some African intervals, while mobile lifeways, tropical decay, river erosion and sparse excavation leave major gaps elsewhere. These records are selected site sequences, not a uniform timeline or population map.

## Africa

Jebel Irhoud in Morocco provides an early North African fossil record relevant to the evolution of Homo sapiens. The fossils date to 315 ± 34 thousand years ago and combine early and derived anatomical features. Omo I in Ethiopia is a separate, eastern African fossil record: correlation of the KHS Tuff with the 233 ± 22 ka Shala eruption gives the fossil a minimum age older than about 233 ka, rather than a precise date. These are evolutionary findings, not evidence of named societies or political geography. Together with the wider African fossil record, they counsel against reducing human origins to one present-day country or a single point on a game map. [Jebel Irhoud paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature22336), [Omo I dating paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04275-8)

In eastern Africa, the Olorgesailie Basin record documents a local shift from a very long Acheulean sequence to Middle Stone Age technologies between approximately 320 and 295 ka. Nonlocal obsidian and worked pigments indicate movement of materials over substantial distances and pigment use. This is important evidence for mobility and social connection, but it does not demonstrate formal markets, permanent trade routes, named identities, or a specific symbolic meaning. In southern Africa, Klasies River preserves a long, intermittent coastal record: cave use and extensive marine-resource exploitation are documented from roughly 120,000 to 59,000 years ago. At Blombos, engraved ochre plaques dated about 77,000–75,000 years old demonstrate deliberate geometric marking; their meaning and any religious interpretation remain unknown. [Olorgesailie research](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao2646), [SAHRA on Klasies River](https://www.sahra.org.za/klasies-river-cave/), [Smithsonian on Blombos](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/behavior/recording-information/blombos-ocher-plaque)

Holocene climate and economy require another distinction: wetter landscapes made new settlement and mobility patterns possible, but climate alone does not specify what a community did. The African Humid Period, broadly about 15–5 ka BP, supported lakes, grasslands and other wetter habitats across parts of the Sahara and Sahel before aridification. The transition was geographically uneven and the interval straddles the 3000 BCE cutoff. West Africa's Ounjougou sequence in Mali includes pottery and stone tools before 9400 cal BCE, in a setting where people intensively gathered wild grasses. This is an important early pottery and foraging record, not proof of farming. [African Humid Period study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-024-47921-1), [Ounjougou paper](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/emergence-of-pottery-in-africa-during-the-tenth-millennium-cal-bc-new-evidence-from-ounjougou-mali/EC383CF3E1B961102319433214C6E034)

In the eastern Sahara, Nabta Playa and Bir Kiseiba preserve human use of seasonal wetland basins. The proposition that cattle there were independently domesticated locally is contested, so the record treats wetland use and possible pastoral contexts as evidence while leaving the domestication claim unresolved. In the Lake Turkana Basin, domestic livestock appear around 5000–4500 cal BP; early herders also fished, so an abrupt shift from foraging to specialist pastoralism is not supported. This livestock interval crosses the cutoff, with its earliest edge just before 3000 BCE and most of the interval after it. Sheep and goats appear farther south in the Rift Valley around 4200 cal BP. These changes support a developing, varied food economy, not a ready-made ethnic or political map. [Nabta reassessment](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10963-017-9112-9), [East African herder evidence](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6827346/)

The Nile Valley supports stronger claims about local communities and, later, political competition. In Upper Egypt, Naqada I begins about 4000 BCE, with small farming settlements along the Nile. Toward the end of the Predynastic period, around 3300 BCE, regional rulers competed for territory and power. This is evidence for political consolidation processes, not sufficient grounds to set unified Egypt's founding earlier than the evidence warrants. The sources used here do not support numerical settlement populations, precise borders, complete ruler lists, or a fully reconstructable belief system before the threshold. [British Museum, Early Egypt](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/galleries/early-egypt)

## The Americas

The first-arrival chronology remains unsettled. White Sands footprints in New Mexico are reported at about 23,000–21,000 years ago. Their initial radiocarbon dating raised a reservoir-effect concern; a 2023 study reported independent pollen and luminescence estimates. This makes the site a major early-presence claim with corroborating methods, while its dating history should remain visible. Cooper's Ferry in Idaho provides a different pre-Clovis line of evidence: a projectile-point assemblage dates to about 15,785 cal BP. These sites reject Clovis as a simple first-arrival marker without resolving the route, number of migration waves, or precise date of first entry. [USGS/Science on White Sands](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70249447), [Cooper's Ferry study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9788777/)

The Clovis complex itself is modeled to approximately 13,050–12,750 cal BP and is recognized by a distinctive lithic tradition over a wide area. It is a technological horizon rather than a polity or ethnicity. In Chile, Monte Verde II is especially consequential because its 14,500 cal BP occupation is supported by a rich assemblage: structures, hearths, tools, plant remains, wooden objects and footprints. A 2023 assessment supports the occupation age while calling for more work on still earlier levels. In March 2026, a Science study argued that the artifact-bearing landform is no older than about 8200 years ago; the excavation leader disputed its sampling and interpretation, and a published multi-author response defended the older evidence. The two claims should remain parallel in the data. Averaging them, or silently replacing one with the other, would manufacture a consensus that does not exist. [Clovis chronology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7577710/), [Monte Verde 2023 assessment](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2023.32), [2026 challenge and response summary](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adw9217)

Early food economies were regionally varied. In Oaxaca, directly dated Cucurbita pepo remains and morphological evidence support domesticated squash at Guilá Naquitz by roughly 10,000 calendar years ago. In the Central Balsas region, maize phytolith and starch evidence at Xihuatoxtla is reported by about 8700 cal BP; the evidence concerns early crop use, not a single instant when domestication was completed. In southwestern Amazonia, archaeobotanical and residue evidence at Teotonio points to early-Holocene plant exploitation and cultivation, while regional research places manioc domestication broadly around 8,000–10,000 years ago. These findings complicate any assumption that agriculture required immediate village settlement or that farming developed on one continent-wide schedule. [Guilá Naquitz study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.276.5314.932), [Balsas Valley study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2664064/), [Teotonio study](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0199868)

Central American evidence helps bridge the continental geography: radiocarbon-dated sequences on the Panamanian isthmus cover roughly 13,000–7,000 BP. The broad sequence supports human movement through the land bridge, but a regional date span is not a route map. Caral-Supe in Peru is included only as boundary context because UNESCO dates its construction to about 3000 BCE. Its later urban and monumental history belongs beyond this class's strict pre-3000 BCE scope. [Panama chronology](https://profiles.si.edu/display/sro_114860), [UNESCO on Caral-Supe](https://whc.unesco.org/document/152221)

## Implications for history data

**Settlements and population:** use named sites and directly evidenced occupation windows. Artifacts, graves, site area, and archaeological-period names do not establish population totals. Mobile groups, seasonal use, repeated visits, and long occupation remain distinct.

**Politics:** evidence supports late Predynastic Egyptian regional competition, not a precise early unification date, ruler list, borders, or continental state tags. Do not turn Clovis or Naqada into states.

**Economy and technology:** evidence is strongest for local subsistence and material practices: coastal and lake resources, wild-grass gathering, pottery, stone tools, obsidian movement, livestock, plant management, and crops. Do not infer networks. Retain the disputed Nabta cattle claim and Monte Verde chronology.

**Belief and conflict:** marking, burial, pigment, animal treatment and monuments do not identify doctrine or institution. At Nataruk, Kenya, 10 of 12 articulated skeletons show trauma interpreted by the original authors as an inter-group attack around 10,000 years ago; a published response contests the massacre interpretation. Keep this claim and its critique visible; it cannot establish a general pattern of warfare. Egyptian evidence supports regional competition, but not a complete conflict history.

The next research priorities are source-level evidence for western and central African Holocene settlement and food economies; site-specific sequences around the Nile south of Egypt; early Central American and Amazonian settlements with directly dated contexts; and current primary publications addressing White Sands and Monte Verde disputes. All location links and numeric population values should be added only after site-level geographic and methodological review.
